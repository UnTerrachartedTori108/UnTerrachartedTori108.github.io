Activity 1, Link 2 (TutorialsPoint: Search Engines) Describe: The article first defines a search engine as a "web-based program or software application" that is "designed to support users in finding information" on the Internet. A user can enter words into the search bar and, based on the search, the search engine (ex: Google, Bing, DuckDuckGo, Yahoo, etc.) will show all relevant information via links, documents, videos, images, etc. Simplistically, the six components of a search engine are described using a web crawler, or sometimes called a spider. It goes as follows; 1. URL Frontier, 2. Fetch Module, 3. Parsing, 4. Content Storage, 5. Indexing, and 6. Duplicate Detection. In a more detailed means of explaining: "It starts working by fetching and examining the URLs, also known as seeds. In the next step, the crawler retrieves the HTML content of the pages specified in URLs. After that, it parses HTML to extract links to another page. It prioritizes pages to fetch next based on criteria like content, originality, importance and crawling policies." Following search engines, an Index is a database where data is organized and saved. An Index is created using 4 steps. Content Processing, which, after receiving content, searches through the web for relevant information. Tokenization, text is divided into "tokens" i.e. words or terms. Normalization, converting tokenized text into "lowercase, stemming, and removing stop words like "and," "the," etc." Index creation, and processed tokens are used to build the index. In relation, an inverted index "includes a list of terms, and for each term, a list of documents.' and Metadata Storage includes document IDs and URLs. Metadata is stored alongside the index. It continues on with the "Functionalities of Search Algorithm," which follows similar ideas to Index functionalities, both look for terms and then showcase relevant info based on search. It then gives examples of common search engines and how they work. Learned: A single search is more involved than I anticipated. I wasn't aware of "tokens" in this context. Describe: The environmental impacts of AI are seemingly widespread and, unfortunately, due to this, there have been environmental impacts on greenhouse gas emissions "from generating electricity for data centers and computing hardware" as well as "operational and upstream water use, material impacts from hardware manufacturing, mining and electronic waste." Though the estimation for actual effects is complicated "because results depend on how impacts are measured, including whether accounting includes only model computation or also data-centre overhead, idle capacity, hardware manufacture, and local electricity supply." As these issues become more of a problem, companies "have increasingly considered" having regulation for AI use. In a nutshell, it was found that" AI contributes to rising electricity demand in data centres and related infrastructure." As for water emission, "Using GPT-3, as an example, they estimated that training the model in Microsoft's US data centers could consume about 700,000 liters of onsite water and about 5.4 million liters in total. They also estimated that 10–50 medium-length GPT-3 responses could consume about 500 mL of water, depending on when and where the model was deployed." That's awful, and "A 2024 study in Nature Computational Science estimated that generative AI could add between 1.2 and 5 million tonnes of e-waste by 2030.
